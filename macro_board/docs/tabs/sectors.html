Sectors, correlation and market psychology

Prices move because people move money. Watching where money goes between sectors, and how sectors move together, is how you read what the crowd is feeling: confident, nervous, or hiding.

Measured on the board's price store: daily returns for the 250 sessions from 17 Oct 2025 to 15 Oct 2026, unless stated. Holdings from the issuer files. These numbers change; the method stays the same.

Why sectors show the crowd's mood

Every day, big investors decide where to keep their money. They rarely leave the market completely. Instead they move between sectors, depending on what they hope for or fear:

  • Confident about growth: money goes to technology, consumer spending, industrials and banks.
  • Nervous: money moves to sectors whose profits hold up in any economy (staples, health care, utilities).
  • Afraid of inflation or a supply shock: money goes to energy and materials.
  • Expecting lower interest rates: money goes to real estate, utilities and homebuilders.

So a sector's move against SPY is a footprint of a decision. Read enough footprints and you can see the crowd's mood before it shows up in the index.

Coach's note AVSHUNTER's edge is to enter before the crowd and sell into it. Sector flows are where the crowd's movement first becomes visible. But the board also measures whether a flow kept going after the same read in the past, and many did not. Psychology gives you the story; the measurement tells you whether to trust it.

Four ideas you need

Correlation

How closely two things move together day to day, from +1 (always together) through 0 (no link) to −1 (always opposite). 0.8 is a strong link; below 0.3 is weak.

Beta

How much a sector moves for each 1% move in SPY. Beta 1.75 means about 1.75% when SPY moves 1%. Beta near 0 means it ignores SPY; negative beta means it tends to go the other way.

Worst-day and best-day capture

What the sector did on SPY's 10% worst and 10% best days, as a multiple of SPY's move. On SPY's worst days SPY fell -1.44% on average; a capture of 0.5 means the sector fell half as much. A negative worst-day capture means it rose. That is where money hides.

Relative correlation

Correlation of a sector's move against SPY with something else, such as oil. It strips out the market and shows what drives a sector to beat or trail it.

The market's mood now

The single best measure of whether the crowd is moving as one is the average correlation between sectors. When fear or euphoria takes over, everything moves together and this number rises. When investors pick and choose, it falls.

Average correlation between the 11 sectors, last 60 sessions0.16
Same measure over the last 250 sessions0.23
Its range since 2021 (60-session windows)low 0.11 · median 0.46 · high 0.78
Where today sitslower than 96% of its history
What this tells you Sectors are moving on their own stories, not as one crowd. Technology still moves with SPY (correlation 0.84), but staples, utilities, health care and energy have largely stopped doing so. In this kind of market, the index can look calm while money moves hard underneath it. Picking the right sector matters more than calling the market. When this number jumps back up, the crowd is moving together again, usually on fear.

The big seesaw Technology sits at one end and almost everything else at the other: XLK's relative move has a correlation of -0.77 with staples, -0.72 with health care and -0.69 with real estate. When money leaves technology, it mostly goes to the defensives, and the reverse. In practice: if you see XLK "Leaving a leader" and XLP, XLV or XLRE "Turning in" on the same day, that is one rotation seen from both ends, not several separate signals.

In 2022 almost every sector moved closely with SPY. In 2026 energy turned negative and the defensives drifted towards zero. The "usual" relationships in textbooks describe an average; the market you trade today can be different.

Correlation is not cause A strong link to oil does not prove oil moved the sector on a given day. It says they have moved together. The board marks these as reasons that fit or go against, never as causes.

Using it in a trade

  1. Read the mood first. Low average correlation: choose sectors carefully, and expect index calm to hide sector moves. High and rising: the crowd is moving together, usually on fear, so size smaller.
  2. Find the rotation. On the Day story tab, look for money leaving one end of the seesaw and arriving at the other. One rotation seen from both ends is stronger than a single sector moving alone.
  3. Check the driver. Does the sector's measured driver agree? XLE moving with oil (relative correlation 0.67) is a coherent story; XLE rising while oil falls is not.
  4. Check the record. "After this read" tells you whether this flow has followed through before. A good story with a "reversed" record is a reason to wait.
  5. Pick the instrument. Beta tells you how hard a sector moves: SMH (beta 2.36) moves far more than XLK (1.75). Higher beta means a bigger move and a bigger loss when you are wrong.
  6. Avoid doubling up. Two ideas in the same family (for example XLY and XLC, correlation 0.64) are close to one bet. Pipeline candidates show their link to their sector ETF in the Ticker impact table.
Coach's summary Sectors tell you what the crowd fears and hopes. Correlation tells you whether the crowd is moving as one. The board's records tell you whether following the crowd has paid. Use all three before you act.
