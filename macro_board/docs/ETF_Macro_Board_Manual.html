AVSHUNTER · trader coaching guide

The ETF Macro Board, explained from the ground up

This guide is written for someone new to trading. It starts with the basic ideas, explains every word before it is used, and then walks you through the board the way a coach sitting next to you would. Read one lesson at a time. You do not need to understand everything on day one.

Rewritten 14 Oct 2026 for novice traders · examples use the board as of the 13 Oct 2026 close unless stated

Start here

Think of the board as a briefing pack, the kind a pilot reads before take-off. It does not fly the plane. It tells you the weather, what changed overnight, and what happened on past days that looked like today. You still make the decision.

The board will

  • Show you what the market did and how it reacted to news.
  • Show what usually happened next after days like today, and how much evidence sits behind that.
  • Check whether a trade idea still makes sense at today's price.
  • Tell you plainly when a number is old, missing or unreliable.

The board will not

  • Place trades, or tell you to buy or sell.
  • Promise that history repeats.
  • Fill a gap with a guess. Missing data is shown as missing.
  • Change the AVSHUNTER pipeline. The board only reads.
Coach's note The most useful skill this board teaches is not prediction. It is knowing how much to trust what you are looking at. Every lesson comes back to that.

Lesson 1 · The building blocks

ETFs

An ETF (exchange-traded fund) is a basket of shares that trades like a single share. Buying one ETF gives you a small piece of everything in the basket. The board follows about 115 of them. Four matter most:

TickerWhat it holdsThink of it as
SPYThe 500 largest US companies (the S&P 500)"The market". Everything on the board is compared with it.
QQQThe 100 largest Nasdaq companies, mostly technologyBig tech
IWMAbout 2,000 smaller US companies (Russell 2000)Small companies, more sensitive to interest rates and the economy
DIA30 large industrial and blue-chip companies (Dow Jones)Old-economy giants

Sector ETFs hold one part of the economy. The 11 main ones start with "XL": XLK technology, XLF financials (banks), XLE energy, XLV health care, XLY consumer spending, XLP consumer staples (food, household goods), XLI industrials, XLB materials, XLU utilities, XLRE real estate, XLC communication. Industry ETFs are narrower still: SMH and SOXX (chip makers), KRE (regional banks), XOP (oil producers), JETS (airlines) and others.

Sessions, returns and horizons

  • A session is one trading day. "5 sessions" means about one week; "20 sessions" about one month.
  • A return is the percentage change in price. "+2% over 5 sessions" means the price is 2% higher than it was five trading days ago.
  • A horizon is how far ahead we look: 1, 5 or 20 sessions on this board.

Relative strength: the most important idea on the board

A sector can go up and still be losing money flow, because the rest of the market went up more. So the board asks two separate questions:

  1. Did it go up or down? That is its own return.
  2. Did it beat SPY? That is its relative return: its return minus SPY's, in "points" (pts). +2 pts means it did 2 percentage points better than the market.
Example. Over 5 sessions XLK fell 2.83% while SPY fell 0.14%. XLK's relative return is about −2.69 pts. Money moved out of technology faster than out of the market.

A "normal day"

Every ETF has its own typical daily move. SPY usually moves about ±0.6% a day; a chip ETF like SMH moves closer to ±1.7%. The board often measures distance in daily moves instead of percent. "A level 2 daily moves away" means "about two ordinary days of movement away", which works for calm and wild ETFs alike.

Coach's tip When you see a percentage, ask "is that big for this ETF?" 1% is a big day for SPY and a quiet day for SMH. Daily moves answer that question for you.

Lesson 2 · Options in plain words

AVSHUNTER expresses its ideas mainly with options, so you need four ideas.

Call and put

A call gains if the price rises. A put gains if the price falls. When you buy one, the most you can lose is what you paid for it: the premium.

Time decay

An option loses value every day that nothing happens. Being right too late can still lose money. That is why the board talks about runway: buy more time than you expect the move to need.

Implied volatility (IV)

The price of an option depends on how much movement the market expects. High IV means options are expensive; low IV means cheap. IV rank (0–100) says where today's IV sits within its past year: 90 is near its most expensive.

Bid, ask and spread

You buy at the ask and sell at the bid. The gap between them, the spread, is a cost you pay every trade. Wide spreads can eat a winning idea.

The straddle: what the market expects

Buying a call and a put at the same price level is a straddle. Its cost tells you how big a move the option market expects. The board compares that with what actually happened after similar days. This gives you "priced vs history":

  • Below 1 (cheap): options expect less movement than history delivered. Buying options is reasonably priced.
  • About 1 (fair).
  • Above 1 (rich): you are paying for more movement than history delivered. You can be right on direction and still lose.
Common mistake Getting the direction right is only half the trade. With options you also need the move to be big enough and soon enough to beat what you paid. Always check "priced vs history" and IV rank before buying.

Lesson 3 · The macro backdrop

Macro means the big forces that move whole markets: interest rates, the economy, money flowing in and out of the system. The board measures ten of them every day and gives each a simple state, such as RISING or FLAT.

ConditionWhat it is, simplyWhy a trader cares
SPY trendIs SPY above its 50-day and 200-day average price?Markets in an uptrend behave differently from markets in a downtrend.
Realised volatilityHow much SPY has actually been moving latelyCalm markets and nervous markets reward different trades.
BreadthHow many stocks are taking part in the moveA rise carried by a few giants is more fragile than one where most stocks rise.
LiquidityHow much money the central bank system is putting in or taking outMore money in the system has tended to support prices.
Curve level and curve moveThe gap between long-term and short-term interest rates, and whether it is widening (steepening) or narrowingBanks and the economy react to the shape of interest rates.
YieldsInterest on 10-year US government bondsHigher yields make borrowing dearer and can weigh on growth stocks.
CreditExtra interest that riskier companies must pay (credit spreads)Widening spreads are an early sign of stress.
DollarThe US dollar against other currenciesA strong dollar hurts exporters and commodities.
OilThe oil price trendMoves energy stocks, airlines, and inflation expectations.
Coach's note Textbooks say things like "rising yields always hurt tech". This board does not assume that. It measures what each ETF actually did in this market when yields were rising. Sometimes the textbook is right; often it is not.

Lesson 4 · How to trust a number

This lesson is the heart of the guide. The board shows thousands of numbers. Five questions tell you how far to trust any one of them.

  1. How many times has this happened? The board counts independent windows: separate, non-overlapping cases. Twenty days in a row of the same condition count as one 20-day window, not twenty. Under about 8 windows, a "?" appears: too little to trust either way.
  2. How consistent was it? An average of +2% from wild swings of −10% and +14% means little. The 80% interval gives a likely range for the true average. If the range includes zero (say −0.3% to +1.5%), even the direction is uncertain.
  3. Did it hold on fresh data? The board sets aside the most recent quarter of history, finds the pattern on the older part, then checks it on the set-aside part. That check is the holdout. ✓ means it held; ✗ means it did not.
  4. Is it better than normal? SPY rises on most 20-day windows anyway. A pattern is only useful if it beats that normal drift (the "base" or "all sessions" figure).
  5. How many things were tested? Test enough ideas and some will look good by pure luck. Where the board runs many tests (Scenario watch), it applies a false-discovery check and marks results "beats the tries" or "within chance".

You will also see t (as in "t +2.0"). It measures how far a result stands out from luck: around 2 or more (or −2 or less) is noteworthy; under 1 is noise.

Scores that look like probabilities Some outside reports give scores such as "75/100", "confidence 90" or "fragility 4.7/5". The board tags these uncalibrated. Someone chose them; nobody has tested them. 75/100 does not mean a 75% chance of anything.

Lesson 5 · How the board thinks

"What happened after days like today?"

The board finds past sessions whose ten macro conditions looked like today's. These are analog days. It then measures what each ETF did over the next 1, 5 and 20 sessions, starting from the next open (the first moment you could actually have traded).

If the result is strong, consistent, passes the holdout and beats normal drift, the board shows a measured lean: ▲ Up or ▼ Down. Otherwise it shows – No clear lean.

"No clear lean" is an answer, not a failure On most days most ETFs show no clear lean. That tells you today's macro gives no measurable edge for that ETF. The trade must stand on its own setup and price. Do not make up a direction from a macro story.

Three voices, always labelled

Some text on the board is measured; some comes from outside reports; some is written by AI. You must always know which you are reading.

MEASURED

Built only from the board's own numbers. The most trustworthy voice, within the limits of Lesson 4.

PUBLISHED · not verified

News and opinions from outside reports, some written by another AI. Treat them as claims to check, not facts.

AI NARRATIVE

Claude writes a readable story using only the measured facts. The board then checks every number it used and every direction call. Green ticks mean the checks passed.

Where the trade ideas come from

The trade ideas themselves (which stock, call or put, target, stop level) come from the AVSHUNTER pipeline, a separate system. The board's job is to check those ideas against the backdrop, history and today's price. The board never feeds anything back into the pipeline.

Lesson 6 · Your daily routine

Getting a fresh board

The full package (all the macro inputs, then the board) runs from PowerShell in the repo folder:

cd C:\Users\ACKVerissimo\AVSHUNTER-Intelligence
.\Run-MacroInputs.ps1 -Session PreOpen     # morning: waits until 08:35 ET
.\Run-MacroInputs.ps1 -Session PostClose   # evening: waits until 16:30 ET

Add -PlanOnly to see what it would do without running anything. If you only want to rebuild the page from inputs that are already fresh, .\Run-MacroBoard.ps1 rebuilds the board alone. The page is saved at macro_board\output\etf_macro_board.html; you can reopen it at any time.

Run-MacroBoard.ps1 is not the full package It does not refresh the macro inputs. If the board looks out of date, run Run-MacroInputs.ps1.

The 15-minute morning routine

  1. Check the input strip under the banner. ✓ fresh, ! stale (old), × missing. Note anything stale and don't lean on the panels that use it.
  2. Daily review: what happened yesterday, and how did the market react to the news?
  3. Day story: where is money going, and why might that be? Read the AI narrative first for the overview, then the measured table.
  4. Next session plan: the market's possible paths for today, the levels that would confirm each one, and the checklist.
  5. Desk card: work through each pipeline idea with the six questions (Lesson 8).
  6. Write it down: your decision and your reason, before the open.
Coach's tip Writing the reason down before the open is the habit that improves traders fastest. After a week you will see which kinds of reasons worked for you and which did not.

Lesson 7 · The tabs, one by one

The tabs are in the order a trader normally uses them. Each has the same four parts: what it answers, where to look first, a coach's tip and a common mistake.

Daily review

Answers: What happened, and did the market believe the news?

  • Look first at the reading per instrument: accepted (the price moved with the news and held), rejected (it opened one way and reversed), or absorbed (little reaction).
  • Then read "what changed and what was expected", and the decision points: scheduled events such as inflation data (CPI) or a Federal Reserve meeting (FOMC) that could move the market in the next session.
  • Lower down, "where the opportunity is strongest" compares QQQ with SPY, sectors with SPY, and how far each price is from its nearest obstacle (recent high or low, 50/200-day averages).

Coach's tip

A good-news day where the price fell (rejected) often tells you more than the news itself.

Common mistake

Reading a headline and assuming the price will follow. Check the reaction first.

Day story

Answers: Where is money going today, what reasons fit, and what does that mean for trades?

  • The day ahead: the market call, scheduled releases before the next close, and three paths for each index ETF: holds the range, breaks up, breaks down.
  • The story in words (AI NARRATIVE): a readable summary. Check the green ticks at the top.
  • Where money is going: one row per sector or industry. Flow labels mean:
    • Money in: beating SPY this week and holding up over the month;
    • Turning in: beating SPY this week after a weak month;
    • Money out: trailing SPY this week and over the month;
    • Leaving a leader: trailing this week after leading all month.
    Volume above 1× means the move had real size behind it. Click a row to see the reasons, the trade analysis and the tickers.
  • Reasons are marked fits or against. They are measured links, not proven causes: "this sector has tended to trail when yields rise, and yields are rising".
  • Options shows what option traders paid for, compared with that ETF's normal activity (heavy put buying, rising implied volatility). Where the stored option data is too erratic to trust, it says so.
  • Trade analysis for the day: the side (call or put), the trigger level that would start the trade, the "wrong if" level that would prove it wrong, whether options are cheap or rich, and "After this read".
  • Ticker impact: each pipeline idea checked against its sector's flow: with it, against it, or "stock-specific" (the stock barely moves with its sector).

Coach's tip

Always read "After this read" before following a flow. It shows what usually happened next after the same flow. On 13 Oct, leaders that started to lose ground (XLK, SMH, SOXX) had more often bounced back than kept falling.

Common mistake

Treating a strong week as a trend that must continue. Most flows on this board have not reliably continued. That is measured, not opinion.

Next session plan

Answers: What could happen today, and what would tell me which path we are on?

  • The call: what the index ETFs did, whether participation was broad or narrow, and a suggested approach for that kind of market (for example "two-way market: trade the edges with smaller size").
  • Scenarios for SPY, QQQ, IWM and DIA: base (holds the range), up and down. Each has a trigger level, a next target level and the expression (calls, puts, or stand aside).
  • A time-ordered checklist for the session, and "what would change the view".
  • The jigsaw: every input with what it points to, whether it agrees with the market, and how fresh it is.
  • ETF by ETF and pipeline candidates read through their sector ETF.

Coach's tip

Decide in advance what you will do at each trigger level. Planning before the open stops you reacting emotionally during it.

Common mistake

Taking a trade inside the range when the plan says "no new position until a level breaks".

Overview

Answers: What is the backdrop, and what changed?

  • The four index cards: the pipeline's idea, the measured lean, and what options charge.
  • The ten condition tiles, each with its state, value, history and the date it was observed. Some government data is published a day or more late; that is normal.
  • What changed in the last 10 sessions. A change of state is often more useful than the level itself.
  • Analog depth: how many past days matched today, and how closely. Fewer matches means weaker evidence.

Coach's tip

Start each morning with "what changed?". It keeps you focused on new information.

Common mistake

Ignoring the observation date and acting on data that is days old.

Desk card

Answers: Should I take this specific trade idea, at today's price?

One card per pipeline idea and per index ETF. The checks strip shows, for each idea:

  • whether macro history and momentum agree with it;
  • room left: reward left to the target against risk left to the stop level, from today's price;
  • the option price, data freshness and event risk.

Below that are the six questions (Lesson 8) already filled in, and a box for your decision: Act, Wait, Skip, or Insufficient information. Download record saves it.

Coach's tip

"Wait" and "Skip" are good decisions. Most professional traders skip far more ideas than they take.

Common mistake

Measuring room from the price the idea was first proposed at, rather than from today's price.

Sector rotation

Answers: Which parts of the market are gaining or losing favour?

  • The rotation map places each sector by its relative strength against SPY and whether that is improving. Sectors tend to travel clockwise: improving → leading → weakening → lagging.
  • Participation: how much of a sector's weight is above its 50-day average. A leading sector with low participation is carried by a few names and is fragile.
  • A ranking of how sectors did against SPY after days like today.

Coach's tip

Use rotation to choose which ETF to express a view with, not as a signal on its own. Measured on this market, "improving" became "leading" only about 20% of the time.

Common mistake

Assuming the clockwise cycle always completes.

Scenario watch

Answers: My trading idea: has it actually worked before?

A scenario is a trade reason written down in advance, such as "QQQ cannot hold a yearly high for 3 sessions, so buy puts". The board checks it on every past occurrence for every ETF.

  • Triggered: the condition was met on the last close. Watching: one step away, with the exact level and deadline. Partial: the price part is met but a macro condition is not.
  • The record shows what a bought option on that side returned after every past trigger, after costs, compared with buying the same option on any day.
  • Verdicts:
    • Edge: it worked;
    • Opposite side paid: the idea was wrong;
    • No edge;
    • Too thin: not enough cases.
  • Only results marked "beats the tries" survive the luck check (Lesson 4, question 5).

Coach's tip

Register a scenario before you look at its result, and keep it even if it fails. That keeps the record honest.

Common mistake

Acting on a "within chance" result as if it were proven.

ETF matrix

Answers: How does every ETF look, side by side?

One row per ETF: returns, strength against SPY, trend, volatility, measured lean, the pipeline's idea, IV rank and "priced vs history". Use the 1d / 5d / 20d buttons to change horizon, click a column to sort, and click a row to open the drawer with the full story for that ETF.

Coach's tip

Sort by "RS vs SPY 20d" to see at a glance which areas are strong and weak.

Common mistake

Using multi-day history on leveraged or volatility products (TQQQ, SQQQ, UVXY, VXX). They reset daily, so the board marks them "Path-dependent".

Transmission map

Answers: Which macro conditions actually move which ETF?

A grid of ETFs against the ten conditions. Blue means the ETF did better while that condition was in today's state; red means worse. Faded cells are weak and should be ignored.

Coach's tip

Use it to replace a textbook belief with a measurement.

Common mistake

Adding cells together. Conditions overlap (yields and the curve move together), so each cell is a separate comparison.

Macro intelligence

Answers: What are the outside reports saying?

The outside macro reports as they were published: the macro packet, the US Money Index, event and news reports, volatility, dealer positioning estimates, rates and credit. Each card shows its source, time and age. The board's own measured Money Index sits first, next to the published one.

Coach's tip

Check the date of every number you quote. A report written today can contain a price from weeks ago.

Common mistake

Reading "uncalibrated" scores as probabilities.

Scorecards and Method

Answers: Have these sources been right? How exactly is each number made?

Scorecards grade every source of macro opinion against what SPY and QQQ did next, including the board itself. A "?" means too few cases to judge. Check it weekly. Method holds every definition and threshold: read it once, then come back when a number surprises you.

The board's history covers about five years (since Aug 2021). That is one interest-rate cycle, so treat every pattern as evidence, not law.

Lesson 8 · From reading to a decision

Before any trade, answer these six questions in writing. The Desk card fills in most of them for you.

QuestionIn plain wordsWhere to look
1. What changed?What is genuinely new since yesterday?Overview → What changed; Daily review
2. What should it affect?Which ETF or stock, and through what?Transmission map; Day story reasons
3. What is the market doing with it?Did the price accept or reject it? Is money flowing in or out?Daily review reading; Day story flow
4. What does the pipeline propose?The idea: call or put, target, stop levelDesk card; ETF drawer
5. Is it still worth taking at today's price?Room left vs risk; are the options cheap or rich?Desk card checks; priced vs history; IV rank
6. What would change my mind?The level or event that proves me wrong"Wrong if" level; decision points

Then write one sentence, filling in the blanks:

"[What changed] affects [ETF] through [reason]. The market is [accepting / rejecting] it and money is [flowing in / out]. The pipeline proposes [trade]. At today's price there is [room] against [risk], and options are [cheap / fair / rich]. I will [act / wait / skip] because [reason]. I will change my mind if [level or event]."

Lesson 9 · Worked examples

Coaching examples. They show how to reason; they are not recommendations.

1 · Energy: money is flowing in, but check the detailsDay story · Turning in

What the board showed (13 Oct close). XLE beat SPY by +5.55 pts over 5 sessions after trailing over 20. Oil (USO) rose 5.64% over the same 5 sessions, and XLE's relative return has moved closely with oil (correlation +0.79). The reasons: oil fits. But share volume was only 0.81× its average, and "After this read" showed no clear follow-through in history.

Coach: "There's a clear story here, and the measurement backs the link to oil. But ask yourself: is this flow real size or a light-volume drift? 0.81× says light. And history gives no edge to following this kind of flow. So the energy view depends on oil. If you want it, your 'wrong if' should include oil reversing, not just XLE's price level. A light-volume move into a 252-day high is a reason to wait for confirmation, not to chase."

2 · Technology: the market leader stumblesDay story · Leaving a leader

What the board showed. XLK trailed SPY by 2.69 pts over 5 sessions after leading by 4.10 over 20. The trade analysis suggested puts below the 50-day average, but "After this read" said reversed. After the same flow in the past, XLK beat SPY by 0.39 pts on average over the next 5 sessions (t +2.0), and kept falling only 38% of the time. Options were rich (priced 1.29× history).

Coach: "This is exactly where new traders get caught. The story sounds bearish: the leader is losing ground. But in this market, leaders that stumble have more often bounced back. And the puts are expensive. So the measured case is against chasing puts here. The honest decision is 'skip', or wait for the 50-day average to break with heavy volume."

3 · A scenario that triggered: "QQQ failed at a yearly high"Scenario watch · 9 Oct

What the board showed. QQQ and SPY could not hold a new yearly high for 3 sessions, a classic "puts" setup. QQQ's record: over the next 5 sessions a put returned −36% on average, against −17% for a put bought on any day. QQQ actually rose 0.75% on average after these triggers, in 6 of the last 8 cases. Verdict: Opposite side paid, but within chance.

Coach: "The textbook says 'failed high, buy puts'. This market's history says that has not paid. It is not strong enough to bet the other way either. Use the trigger as a moment to look harder at the setup, not as a trade."

4 · A run that kept going: QQQ after 5 up daysMomentum history

What the board measured (Aug 2021 – Oct 2026). After 5 or more up closes in a row, QQQ rose +2.86% over the next 20 sessions on average, against +1.18% for all sessions (22 windows). For SPY: +2.19% against +0.92%.

Coach: "Many people say 'it's gone up five days, it must be too late'. Here the measurement says runs have more often continued. Not always, and mostly in a rising market, but enough that 'it's extended' is not a reason on its own to stay out. Check Momentum history for the specific ETF instead of assuming."

Lesson 10 · Habits, rules and red flags

Good habits

  • Start from the pipeline's idea; use the board to check it.
  • Measure room from today's price.
  • Check "priced vs history" and IV rank before buying options.
  • Read "After this read" before following any flow.
  • Check the date of every number you use.
  • Write your decision and reason before the open.
  • Review your notes and the Scorecards once a week.

Avoid

  • Treating uncalibrated scores as probabilities.
  • Acting on a lean whose holdout says ✗.
  • Treating "?" or "within chance" as proof.
  • Assuming a run is "too late", or that it must continue. Measure it.
  • Treating PUBLISHED news as fact.
  • Using multi-day history on leveraged or volatility products.
  • Counting two reports of the same event as two reasons.
Stop and ask your desk lead when
  • an input you rely on is red or stale;
  • the pipeline and the measured evidence point in opposite directions;
  • the option spread is very wide (above about 5% of the straddle price);
  • the AI narrative shows an amber or red check;
  • the index is rising while breadth or participation collapses.

Check yourself

Try each one before opening the answer.

1. XLF rose 1% this week. SPY rose 2%. Is money flowing into financials?

No. XLF's relative return is about −1 pt. It went up, but it lost ground to the market. Relative strength (Lesson 1) is what tells you about money flow.

2. A lean shows ▲ Up with "holdout ✗". What do you do?

Don't rely on it. The pattern did not hold on the most recent, set-aside history (Lesson 4).

3. You are sure SPY will rise, but "priced vs history" is 1.4 and IV rank is 88. What is the risk?

The options are expensive. SPY can rise less than the option price assumes, and the call still loses (Lesson 2).

4. A sector shows "Leaving a leader", and "After this read" says "reversed". What does that mean?

In the past, after this same flow, the sector more often recovered against SPY than kept falling. Following the flow with puts is against the evidence (Lessons 7 and 9).

5. A report says "risk score 75/100". What is the chance of a sell-off?

Unknown. The score is uncalibrated; it is not a probability (Lesson 4).

6. The Overview shows "No clear lean" for QQQ at every horizon. Does that mean "don't trade QQQ"?

No. It means today's macro adds no measurable edge. Judge the trade on its own setup, the room left and the option price (Lesson 5).
